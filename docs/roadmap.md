# Roadmap

Status of pre(f)light and what comes next. Effort sizes are rough guesses: **S** is hours, **M** a day or two, **L** several days. Rows below the "Done" section say what part of them is already built.

pre(f)light is mainly a **planning tool**, not a live visualizer. Image quality and correctness come before frame rate; 20-30 fps with a large rig is acceptable, especially during development. Using it live is a bonus, so features that cost frame time are fine if they make the picture better, as long as the view stays usable while moving.

## Done

- **Venues:** SketchUp (`.skp`) to GLB converter (geometry, textures, material inheritance); a Venues window (opened from the venue name at the top of the sidebar, so the lights, groups and views below it visibly belong to the open venue) with create, upload, switch, rename, drag-to-reorder, delete (after a confirmation that names what goes with it) and replace-model (the new file is converted on the side and only swapped in when that works, so a bad file leaves the venue as it was; lights, groups and views stay where they are); haze and room light saved per venue (a venue made before this adopted the values in use at the time, a new one starts from the defaults); a Duplicate button that copies a whole venue (model, lights, groups, crowd, haze, room light and views, listed right after the original and opened); lights, groups and crowd zones copied between venues.
- **Camera:** interior fly camera, saved camera views per venue (keys 1-9), camera position remembered per venue, FOV setting, "Overview" and "Inside" jumps.
- **Atmosphere:** haze and room-light settings.
- **Fixtures:** moving heads placed by clicking on any surface (the base aligns to the surface), dimmer, color, zoom, pan and tilt, full 3D rotation with Shift snapping to 5 degrees (tick marks and a live readout), duplicate, delete, rename.
- **Rendering performance:** clustered lighting (120+ lights at about 57 fps on an M1 Pro, versus about 11 fps with plain lights, measured before the post-processing below), native display resolution, adaptive resolution while things move (targets 30 fps). Two settings, both as a fraction of the display's native resolution per axis (1/1, 3/4, 2/3, 1/2, 1/3 or 1/4; 1/2 renders a quarter of the pixels): the size when still (native by default) and the lowest size while moving (1/4 by default, never above the size when still). The size steps in discrete notches (divisors 1, 1.25, 1.5, 2, 2.5, 3, 4 of the resting size), so a floor lands on the nearest notch at or above it (3/4 lands at 0.8). Old saved settings are migrated: the former native-resolution switch becomes the size when still.
- **Focus:** each moving head has a focus distance (2-40 m, in the inspector). The beam edge is sharpest there and feathers inwards toward the lens and beyond, by a blur that grows with the distance from the focus. The spotlight on surfaces takes the softness at the distance where the beam hits something (measured with a ray when the light changes), because a spotlight cannot vary its edge with distance, so every surface hit by one light shares one edge softness. Lights and venue materials use the glTF falloff (inverse-square, with a cone that fades between an inner and an outer angle): Babylon's physical falloff is a fixed bell shape that ignores the inner angle, so no spot edge could ever be sharp, and a per-light falloff type overrides the material's, so both must be glTF. Read the edge off a rendered row of pixels to judge it: on a dark floor the bell-shaped specular and low albedo hide the real cone edge. Gobos will reuse this. *Fixtures without a focus channel* (tubes, bars, pars, most washes) have no optics to sharpen the edge, so their edge has a fixed softness that grows with the beam angle (10% of the beam radius for a narrow beam, up to 45% for a very wide one, about 36% for a 120-degree tube), in both the haze and the light on surfaces.
- **Look:** tone mapping (Neutral or ACES) with an exposure slider, a surface-light slider (stops) that scales surface illumination separately from the beams in haze, bloom, haze-driven fog, glowing lenses, MSAA anti-aliasing, beam brightness handled in linear light.
- **Frame-rate counter:** bottom right, refreshed once a second, switchable in the settings; it also shows the render size when adaptive quality has lowered it.
- **Export:** high-res screenshot, always exactly 16:9 (1x, 2x or 4x the view's pixel density, capped by GPU limits, width a multiple of 16), taken from the live canvas at a higher internal resolution, so it includes bloom, fog and tone mapping. It is the middle part of the view: the canvas is resized to the 16:9 shape for the capture (width and height set separately), and for a window narrower than 16:9 the camera's vertical field of view is reduced so the picture keeps the window's horizontal view. A frame guide in the viewport that shows the cropped area is not built.

## Rendering improvements

These are ideas, none are built. They are grouped by effort. Babylon supports the building blocks, but none of them have been tried in this project, and a few cost real frame time, so each should get the same before-and-after check that was done for clustered lighting.

### Quick wins (S)

1. **Tone mapping and an exposure slider.** *Done.* Neutral (hue-true) and ACES, exposure in stops. Real photometric units would build on this later.
2. **Bloom and lens glow.** *Done.* Bloom with a strength slider, and lenses rendered brighter than white so they glow.
3. **Anti-aliasing pass.** *Done* as 4x MSAA in the render pipeline (FXAA is used instead for the very largest screenshots).
4. **Atmospheric fog.** *Done.* Fog density follows the haze slider and its color rises slightly with the room light.
5. **Texture filtering.** *Done.* Venue textures use 16x anisotropic filtering (the GPU's limit applies if lower). Measured on the sample venue the effect is small: about 0.4 % of pixels change noticeably, and 4x looks identical to 16x there, because the one brick texture is low resolution and mostly seen at moderate angles.

### Medium (M)

6. **Beams that end at surfaces.** *Done.* The beam shader reads a depth pass of the scene (always on, because the beam is occluded with it) and stops each beam at the first surface, fading it out over the last 0.6 m so there is no hard elliptical edge on the floor. The fade can be switched off under Quality ("Soft beam edges at surfaces"), which gives a hard cut. A beam through an opening such as an arch or a door ends at the model's bounding box (the haze stops there), not at the opening itself.
7. **Better haze look.** *Mostly done.* Beams are no longer shaded cones. Each pixel integrates the light along its view ray through the exact beam shape (a cone that starts at the lens radius and widens at the zoom angle), so there are no facets (the old stripes came from flat shading per triangle, not from aliasing), the edge is soft by construction, a beam looks brighter when you look down it, it is occluded and cut by surfaces using the depth map, and it works with the camera inside the beam. *Haze wisps:* the haze in the beams varies in space and drifts over time (a 3D noise field fixed in the room, so beams that cross see the same haze), with a "Haze wisps" slider in the settings (35% by default, 0 for an even haze). The beam samples it at each step along the ray, so strong wisps are averaged out along long beams. *Strip-shaped lenses:* bars and panels have a beam that is a flat sheet, not a cone: the lens strip swept outward, with round ends. The shader finds where a ray is inside it as the union of the two end cones and the slab between them, and the beam hull follows the same outline. Measured with an isolated bar beam: 0.77 m wide seen from the front and 0.47 m seen end-on at 1.5 m height, against about 1.6 times as wide predicted. The light on surfaces is a sheet too: 3 or 5 spot lights share the fixture's output along the strip. Bars and panels do not cast shadows (their lights would leak through blockers). *Still open:* beams that read brighter in a darker room, and non-round beams for other fixtures (rectangular framing, lasers, multi-beam effects).
8. **Consistent beam colors.** The beam shader skips the scene's color and tone pipeline, so beam and room colors can disagree slightly; route it through the same pipeline.
9. **Gobos.** Done, in the beam and on surfaces (see the fixture library row). These fixtures need regular lights instead of clustered ones, because clustered lights cannot carry textures.
10. **Venue materials.** SketchUp provides flat colors: set roughness and metalness by material type, and add a faint environment light so surfaces get a gradient instead of flat fill.
11. **Contact shadows and ambient occlusion.** Darkening in corners and under objects.
12. **Better fixture models.** Real shapes instead of box, yoke and cylinder, plus instancing so hundreds of fixtures stay cheap.
13. **Smoother geometry.** *Investigated.* The vault is faceted in the SketchUp model itself: the edges between its strips are hard (15-45 degrees apart and not marked soft or smooth), so SketchUp shows it faceted too. Smoothing that *is* marked in the model (about 33,000 soft/smooth edges, mostly curved parts at 5-15 degrees) is already applied, because the SDK's mesh helper bakes it into its normals; averaging it again in the converter changed only 280 normals and was removed. If you want the vault to look round in the previews, the option is an optional "auto smooth" in the converter that treats edges below a chosen angle (say 25 degrees) as smooth. That would make the preview differ from SketchUp, so it is not done. A rounder silhouette would need the model itself to be finer, or subdividing curves. The converter also duplicates repeated components instead of instancing them.

### Larger (L)

14. **True volumetric haze.** Raymarched light shafts that react to geometry and occlusion are the real realism step, but expensive; would need a "high quality" toggle.
15. **Reflections.** Glossy floors and stage surfaces reflecting the beams (screen-space reflections or a reflection probe).
16. **Real shadows.** *Done for a limited set of lights (10 at once, tubes and bars included: a light that casts shadows has its shadow map on the middle light of its lens, so a strip-lens fixture such as a Titan Tube is lit from its middle while it casts shadows, instead of by the several lights along it; its haze gets its own lens view of the scene, with the people in it, up to an 80 degree half field of view, and the crowd's light-absorbing boxes are then left out for that beam). A light's own body is left out of its shadow map (its light starts on or in it; a tube's body shadowed about a quarter of the room), while the bodies of other fixtures, the venue and the crowd are in it. Lighting a tube from its middle light alone matches the old five lights (the room measured within 2 % with shadow maps that had no casters), so what changes is only the shadows. Note for anyone changing the lights: a light inside the clustered container keeps shining when it is disabled, so a light that must not shine gets an intensity of 0, not just `setEnabled(false)`; a shadow map is rendered again whenever the light's cone angle changes (the measured aim, a couple of frames after load, changes the soft edge and so the cone: a map rendered before that no longer matches the cone the light looks its shadows up with, which cropped the lit surface to a rectangle until a light was nudged).* Up to 6 lights cast shadows (a per-light "Casts shadows" option, first come first served, plus a master switch). Those lights leave the clustered system and become regular lights with a 1024 px shadow map, so venue surfaces get real shadows (the DJ's shadow on the riser, the floor under a table). Each of them also gets a depth view from its lens, which the beam shader looks up at every sample along the view ray, so the haze is shadowed too: beams are blocked by objects and show light shafts between the table legs. Shadow maps render only when something changed: the fixture being edited re-renders its shadows (map and haze view) every frame, so they follow it without lag (checked at 60 fps with 3 lights while sweeping a pan), and all the others refresh once about 150 ms after the edits stop, which catches shadows cast by a moved fixture's body. Measured with 13 lights (6 casting) at native resolution: about 1-3 fps cost. *Limits:* clustered lights, which are all the others, cannot cast shadows; with many shadow casters being edited at once the per-frame cost adds up. *Shadows in the haze:* each view ray is cut into 16 pieces whose boundaries are shifted per pixel, and the exact position of a shadow edge inside a piece is found by repeated halving, so large shadows (a table, a person) have crisp edges and no banding. Thin shadows, much narrower than a piece (table legs, far from the lens), show up as fine grain instead of clean sheets, because a piece is only tested at its ends. A first version sampled 14 points per ray with a deliberate blur on the lookup, which gave blocky, tile-like shadow edges, so it was replaced.
17. **Photographic camera model.** Depth of field, vignette, and an ISO / shutter / aperture exposure for screenshots.

## Known limits today

- **Light brightness is approximate.** Beam intensity is compressed with a square root so zoom changes stay visible without clipping; it is not a calibrated photometric match.
- **Clustered lights cannot use gobo textures or shadows.** Only up to 10 lights cast shadows and up to 4 project a gobo onto surfaces (they are regular lights, outside the clustered system), so in a big rig most lights are unshadowed and most gobos show in the haze only.
- **Beams fade softly at surfaces and end at the model's bounding box:** through an opening that lies inside the box, a beam still continues for its full length (25 m).
- **Exposure, bloom and the other viewer settings are global** and stored in the browser, not saved per venue (haze and room light are saved with each venue).
- **The look depends on the display pipeline:** bloom's spread is set in pixels, so a very large screenshot has proportionally tighter glow than the live view.
- **Fixture bodies are simple procedural shapes**, not manufacturer models. Each is built from its kind (moving head with yoke, scanner, can, panel, bar, box) and the real size from the library (a default size where it has none), with the lens and pan/tilt pivots placed from those proportions. Real GDTF models (from GDTF Share, whose reuse terms I could not confirm) could replace them one fixture at a time later. The catalog gives each fixture its real beam angles, travel and lumens (estimated from power when the library has none), but gobos, prisms, iris, frost, framing shutters and effects are not simulated yet, and only bars and panels get a non-round beam (a flat sheet): pars, strobes, lasers and effects are round cones from a disc-shaped lens. Where the library has no lens data, the beam angle is a guess.
- **The converter ignores colorized-texture tinting** and duplicates repeated SketchUp components in the output.
